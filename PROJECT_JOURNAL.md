# Project Journal

## Context

I worked with Fixstant as Project Manager and Operations Lead from September 2024 to February 2026. The product served several groups with different needs: clients looking for trusted help, artisans looking for clear work opportunities, marketers supporting growth, and operations teams responsible for quality and resolution.

The central challenge was not simply designing a booking page. It was creating a service journey that could remain understandable when price, availability, materials, timing, or trust changed.

## Challenge 1: Turning informal requests into useful briefs

Home service requests often begin with incomplete information. A client may understand the problem but may not know the technical language needed to explain it. An artisan, however, needs enough context to judge the work and prepare a credible quotation.

The workflow therefore collects the preferred date and time, service location, a description of the issue, and supporting photographs. The artisan can ask questions before committing to a price. This reduces guesswork and gives both parties a shared starting point.

The lesson was simple: the quality of the quotation depends on the quality of the brief.

## Challenge 2: Building trust before asking for payment

Clients wanted to understand who would visit their home and what the work might cost. Artisans needed confidence that serious requests would lead to timely payment.

The operating flow addresses this by placing profile evaluation, clarification, quotation, and agreement before payment. Ratings, location, experience, reviews, portfolios, and service listings help clients make a more informed choice. The quotation then gives both sides a visible scope before money moves.

The lesson was that payment should follow understanding and agreement, not replace them.

## Challenge 3: Making negotiation manageable

Negotiation is a normal part of many local service transactions, but an open conversation can become difficult to track. Labour, materials, timing, and scope may change at different moments.

The workflow separates important quotation components and keeps negotiation within a clear review process. Materials remain visible, while the flexible part of the quotation can be discussed without losing the original job context.

The lesson was that negotiation needs structure. A conversation becomes operationally useful only when the final agreement is reflected in the shared record.

## Challenge 4: Designing for problems, not only success

A smooth booking flow is only one part of marketplace operations. The harder work begins when an artisan is unavailable, a client changes the scope, material prices change, an appointment is missed, or either party disputes completion.

I treated these situations as expected operating cases rather than rare exceptions. Each case needs an owner, a next action, a record, and a clear point of escalation.

The lesson was that trust is often built during recovery. A marketplace becomes reliable when people know what happens next after the normal path breaks.

## Challenge 5: Connecting several operating surfaces

The client, artisan, marketer, and administration experiences serve different purposes. If they operate as isolated tools, teams can lose context between acquisition, booking, service delivery, and support.

The case study presents them as parts of one operating system connected by the service record. Each surface should contribute information to the same journey instead of creating a separate version of the truth.

The lesson was that clear ownership matters, but shared state matters just as much.

## Challenge 6: Presenting the work responsibly

This portfolio project had to show strategic and operational depth without exposing private company information. I did not have access to the production source code, and source code was not required to explain the work.

The public case study uses public pages, supplied workflow documents, and carefully stated professional context. It does not publish private dashboards, customer information, internal percentages, settlement rules, credentials, or confidential commercial logic.

The lesson was that a credible portfolio does not need to reveal everything. It needs to show sound thinking, responsible boundaries, and evidence for every important claim.

## Future AI integration recommendation

During the period covered by this case study, I proposed a future AI integration that could help the team organise enquiries, identify missing details in job briefs, support matching, summarise operational cases, and route sensitive issues to people.

The purpose was to reduce repetitive coordination and help teams respond consistently. Human ownership would remain essential for quotations, payment exceptions, disputes, and other decisions with meaningful consequences.

This was a recommendation rather than an implemented feature.

## What I would measure

If reliable operational data became available, I would evaluate brief completion, time to first artisan response, quotation acceptance, negotiation length, cancellation reasons, completion confirmation time, dispute frequency, and resolution time.

These are suggested measures, not reported Fixstant results.

## Closing reflection

This project strengthened my understanding of marketplace operations. Product decisions, service quality, payment logic, support processes, and team ownership cannot be designed separately. The strongest operating model gives every participant enough context to make the next decision with confidence.
